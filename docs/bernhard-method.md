# Bernhard's practice method: knowledge file for the piano trainer app

Compiled 2026-10-24. Source material is the Piano Street (formerly pianoforum.net) forum, posts by the user "bernhard", 2003-2006 in the threads read here. Everything below is paraphrased except one short quote; a citation like [T3039 r42][T3039] means topic 3039, reply number 42 (the forum's own numbering at the time of reading, counting the opening post as 0). All links are listed in Section 6.

## 1. Who he was, and how this file was compiled

Bernhard was a pseudonymous private piano teacher who lived in the UK ([T3561 r13][T3561]) and wrote more than 5,000 posts on the forum. He taught from his home, played piano and recorder ([T3371 r11][T3371]), and said he works on about 30 pieces a month simply because he teaches them ([T4750 r16][T4750]). His stance: repertoire first, technique extracted from pieces, no abstract exercises (Hanon, Dohnanyi, Cortot's "Rational Principles"), plan everything, and test every claim yourself on two pieces of similar difficulty. He did not call it a method and credited many others (C.C. Chang, Arrau, Richter, Kirkpatrick, Fischer). Nothing here is peer reviewed; his numbers are his own estimates or "statistics" without published data, and he invites A/B testing rather than belief.

## 2. Principles by theme

Format: Rule (paraphrased), numbers, sources, and a caveat where he hedges or it depends on the student.

### 2.1 Chunking and the 7x20 rule

- **Chunk size is found by test, not chosen.** Take a candidate passage, repeat it seven times (hands separate while investigating). If it is learned (right notes, right fingering and rhythm, memorised, feels easy) the size is right. If it is falling apart or you are still reading it, halve it and test again, down to two notes if needed. The seven investigative repeats are not "practice"; they only measure size. Numbers: 7 repeats, halve each time, floor of two notes. Sources: [T4710 r1][T4710], [T3039 r42-43][T3039], [T4105 r8][T4105], [T3561 r2][T3561]. Caveat: he insists on exactly seven (not six or eight) in one post and in another calls it a statistic with a standard deviation of about 2 (68% of students need 5-9, 95% need 3-11) and tells you to find your own number ([T4105 r8][T4105]). Whether the seven repeats are HS or HT was asked directly in [T4710 r3][T4710] and not answered there; [T3039 r43][T3039] says investigate HS so errors do not enter hand memory.
- **More repeats of an oversized chunk do not help.** If seven repeats did not do it, a hundred will not; shrink the chunk. His poem illustration: a 200-line poem read 7 times in 3.5 h teaches nothing, but a one-line chunk learned in 7 repeats (63 s) done 200 times also costs about 3.5 h and works. He claims 7 versus 700 repeats of a right-sized chunk reach mastery in the same number of days and challenges readers to test it. Sources: [T3039 r42][T3039], [T4105 r8][T4105].
- **If it is perfect after the seven repeats, do not practise it.** Move on. The sections that need long practice are the ones that were "learned" but are not secure; those can need practice even while the piece is already being performed. Sources: [T3561 r2][T3561].
- **20 minutes is a ceiling per chunk per day, not a target.** Typical need is 5-10 minutes, 15-20 is normal for a newly tackled chunk, he accepts 30, and he reports managing with sessions as short as 10. If the chunk is not mastered by the ceiling it was too big: halve it. Stop when the aim is reached; do not keep going to fill the time. Exceptions where the cap does not apply: repeated note-group sets (30-60 min, sometimes hours), full run-throughs and performance practice, and a join session that overruns (35 min is fine). Sources: [T4710 r1][T4710], [T3039 r4, r11, r49][T3039], [T4105 r8][T4105], [T4858 r8, r9][T4858].
- **Overlap every chunk with its neighbour** (add the first beat of the next bar, or share a bar) so joins do not stutter. The overlap matters more than anything else when working in small sections. Sources: [T4797 r25][T4797], [T4710 r1][T4710], [T7664 r0][T7664].
- **Hardest passages first, and daily until mastered.** The hardest bars hold all the technique the piece needs and take longest, so start them at once rather than meeting them last (he says 99% of repertoire has them near the end). For one piece he reserves two sessions a day: one for the difficult bars, one for the rest, so the rest advances while the hard part cooks. Source: [T5298 r1][T5298] (8-day example in Section 3), also [T3561 r2][T3561], [T7810 r1][T7810].
- **Absolute size depends on the player and the piece.** A beginner can learn the first 12 bars of Satie's Gymnopedie 1 in one session; the Bach Partita no. 1 prelude may need half a bar; an experienced player can read and memorise the whole Gymnopedie in 10-20 minutes. Sources: [T4797 r25][T4797], [T4710 r1][T4710].
- **Do not practise what is already easy.** Play it, sight-read it, then perform it. Practice is a temporary procedure for passages you cannot yet do. Sources: [T3561 r2][T3561], [T5298 r29][T5298].
- **Expect to judge size by eye after days to weeks** of keeping to the procedure. Source: [T4710 r1][T4710]. His basic landmark there: "Expect progress or evidence of progress." If there is none within minutes, change what you are doing.

### 2.2 The stages, and how to test them

There are several separate "three"s in his posts; they are different axes:

| Axis | Stages | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Level of a piece or chunk | Learned (perfect at end of session, may vanish overnight) -> Mastered (plays reliably, keep it up by performing; relearning after neglect is fast) -> Omniscience (will survive decades of neglect, like riding a bicycle) | [T4750 r14][T4750], [T4689 notes][T4689], [T4105 r8][T4105] |
| Work on a new piece | 1 Exploratory (listening, analysis, sight-read, fingering and movement choices, planning, mostly away from piano) -> 2 Technical ingraining (the 7x20 work, HS then HT, practice tricks, hand memory) -> 3 Interpretation and performance (phrasing, dynamics, rubato, hall, nerves; about the third performance is when you are really ready) | [T3039 r42][T3039], [T4105 r12][T4105] |
| Where a piece sits for scheduling | 1 Impossible, tiny chunks, daily 20-min sessions -> 2 Manageable, memorisation, a "plan B" for blanks, fine-tuning, can be spaced out -> 3 Technically easy, performance logistics | [T4105 r12][T4105] |
| Inside one session | Investigation (find notes, size, movement; seconds to minutes if the chunk is small) -> Ingraining (starts the moment you get it right; this is the bulk of the session) | [T4858 r7][T4858], [T4689 notes][T4689] |

The stages overlap; he says they inform each other (a fingering chosen in stage 1 may be revised in stage 2, and musicality should start as soon as the chunk is big enough, not after the notes) ([T3039 r42][T3039], [T4750 r14][T4750]).

How to test:

- **End of session test:** the chunk should be perfect, memorised and feel effortless. If not, the chunk was too big.
- **Next-morning cold test:** play it first thing. Perfect (or perfect after a few repeats) means move on. If it has "gone", it will feel as if never seen; do not shortcut: redo the previous day's steps in full, because the same procedure works again and takes a fraction of the time. Examples: 63 s -> 45 s -> 18 s -> zero for a one-line chunk ([T3039 r42][T3039]); 20 min -> 6 min -> instant in a student's log ([T4797 r7, r17][T4797]); 15 min -> 2-3 min for Gymnopedie bars 1-12 ([T4710 r1][T4710]). How many retries the cold test allows before you fall back to the full redo is stated three ways: after 4-5 repeats ([T4797 r20][T4797]), after 6-7 repeats ([T4858 r8][T4858]), or simply watch the trend and change approach if things keep falling apart ([T4858 r8][T4858]).
- **Which test counts after joining:** once hands-together from the day before is right on day 2 you do not need to redo the HS sessions that led to it ([T4858 r33][T4858]). Only if the HT fails do you restart from the HS steps.
- **Time to mastery:** about 4 days on average, standard deviation about 2 days (68% in 2-6 days, 95% in 0-8); he says he has never seen anyone need more than seven days for a right-sized chunk, typical 2-3 ([T4105 r8][T4105], [T3039 r4, r43][T3039], [T4797 r20][T4797]).
- **Long-term test for omniscience:** leave the piece alone for 6-12 months. If you cannot play it, you did not master it; relearn from scratch, pretend never seen. After 3-5 cycles it no longer fades. Plan these relearns into the 5-year list ([T4105 r8][T4105], [T4168 r3, r6][T4168], [T5767 index][T5767]).
- **Typical speeds for experts:** someone who can play the Chopin etudes can master the easy preludes (op. 28 nos. 4, 7, 20) in about 20 minutes, a beginner needs two weeks; someone who can play the WTC takes 2-3 days at 40-60 min/day for a two-voice invention, a beginner a month ([T4750 r14][T4750]).
- Honest self-scoring matters: a student who counted "learned" as knowing the notes but could not play it 7 times in a row found he had to halve the chunk ([T4858 r0][T4858]; this is the student's reading, not Bernhard's rule).

### 2.3 Repetition, stopping, fatigue, overnight

- **Stop at your best rendition.** Fatigue (mental and physical) creeps in; mistakes appear; you chase the earlier level and rehearse errors; the brain stores the last repeats, which are the worst. So finish while still perfect ([T4710 r1][T4710]). (From earlier notes: repeat correctly at least as many times as you did it wrong; stop while repeats are still perfect.)
- **One pass at a chunk per day.** Learning is consolidated overnight (he cites dreaming about the piece as the sign it is "taking" and says it is done by the unconscious). So work on a chunk once a day, then leave it until tomorrow, and use remaining sessions for different material. Doing the same chunk 12 times in a day is, in his words, a waste ([T3039 r4, r49][T3039]). Source for the plan form: 12 sessions of 20 min with 5 min breaks is 5 hours, but spread out is better ([T4710 r1][T4710]). Caveat: the mechanism is asserted, not shown; the practical content is spacing.
- **A hurry plan exists** that treats a join as new material on the same day. He allows it but advises against it; his final arbiter is progress: if you are progressing you are doing the right thing ([T7664 r1][T7664]).
- **Never rehearse errors.** Plan the fingering and movement away from the piano first, mentally rehearse before pressing keys, and only repeat correct things (Backhaus is quoted as practising only right notes) ([T3371 r7][T3371]). Mistakes in HS wash out; mistakes in HT enter hand memory ([T4858 r9][T4858]).
- **Protect the hands.** Do not spend a whole session on one hand; alternate (Section 2.5). Limit chromatic figuration work to 2-3 min at a time (earlier notes). Repeated note-group sets alternate hands per group so they can run for hours without strain ([T4858 r9][T4858]).
- **Attention budget.** Roughly seven items fit in consciousness. Drill one aspect at a time with full attention until it is automatic, then move the attention elsewhere; automatic playing then handles about 99% and conscious attention goes to musicality ([T4858 r41][T4858]). Blind repetition is the worst practice; at the small-chunk stage vary how you check it (looking at the keys, eyes closed, eyes on the score, reading) ([T4858 r41][T4858]).
- **Do not believe, test.** Pick two pieces of similar difficulty, learn one his way and one yours, keep a journal, compare after a couple of weeks ([T3039 r41, r43][T3039], [T4797 r20, r25][T4797], [T3085 r15][T3085]). Feeling that it works is not the test.
- **If it is not working, do something else.** He repeats this in many forms ([T4797 r19][T4797], [T5298 r10][T5298]).

### 2.4 Session planning and joining

- **Planning is the real skill.** Break the piece into chunks by analysing its structure first (which bits repeat, where the hard bits are), order them (hard first), and then follow the plan. Sessions devoted only to analysis and planning are legitimate practice. A teacher's main value is saving you this time ([T4710 r1][T4710], [T7810 r12, r25][T7810]).
- **Standard cadence: new chunk, then a join.** Each new small chunk is followed by (or alternates with) a session that joins everything done so far, so every other session is a join. The join is where musicality starts. Concrete cadences in Section 3: Gymnopedie, Invention no. 1, CPE Bach Fantasia, Blow Sarabande, Chopin Cantabile, Op. 10 No. 2 ([T4710 r1][T4710], [T7664 r0][T7664], [T7810 r1][T7810], [T4689 notes][T4689]).
- **A day's rhythm:** start with 2-3 minutes reviewing the previous chunk(s); if instantly right (maybe 3-4 plays), move on; if not, spend the session relearning it fully, and if time remains do the new chunk in a later session the same day ([T4710 r8][T4710], [T7664 r3][T7664], [T7810 r1][T7810]). Large joined sections you already own are reviewed in a couple of minutes (3 plays is his ideal), freeing time for the new chunk ([T7664 r1, r3][T7664]).
- **Sessions per week and per day.** Fewer than three sessions a week means no real progress. Plan for the minimum number of daily sessions you can always manage; on days with more sessions give extra sessions to the same pieces (3 per piece instead of 1) rather than spreading thinly and neglecting pieces. Light days: play large mastered sections and work on musicality; heavy days: the nitty-gritty ([T4797 r21][T4797]).
- **Parallel pieces.** Long term: a 1-5 year list; monthly: 5-30 pieces (10 as an example); daily: 10-15 minutes per piece, any time of day, not necessarily consecutive; tick a pieces-by-days table; replace a piece only at month end even if it was learned in week one; review goals after 2-3 months. Keep working a piece daily until month end, then perform it ([T7810 r0, r1, r2][T7810]). Sessions may also be non-piano work: listening, analysis, mental practice ([T7810 r0][T7810]).
- **Plan as you go.** The student who planned a 55-session spreadsheet up front dropped it for a running log with goal, steps and conclusion per session, planning tomorrow at the end of today; Bernhard confirmed leaving the table blank and filling it in as you go ([T4858 r6, r43, r45][T4858], [T7810 r14][T7810]).
- **Session timelines he gives.** Gymnopedie 1: 10 sessions, about 10 days for a beginner ([T4710 r0][T4710]); Invention no. 1: 39 sessions, 20-40 daily sessions in practice, compressible to 18 or 12 days with several sessions a day ([T7664 r0, r1][T7664]); an 8-session plan for a short Bach Fantasia ([T7810 r1][T7810]).
- **Scheduling around a hard section:** like cooking, put the slowest-cooking part on first, then prepare other parts while it simmers ([T7664 r3][T7664]).
- **Minimum organisation he asks for:** a practice diary (Section 2.10), a plan for the next day written at the end of today, and a piece list rotated for performance.

### 2.5 Hands separate, hands together, dropping notes

- **HT is much harder than HS** (he puts it at about 37 times, give or take 3, then explains with a bicycle-and-shoelace analogy; treat the number as rhetorical). Why: the hands tend to move sympathetically, so each must be inhibited from copying the other. Sources: [T3085 r2, r7, r9][T3085].
- **Two kinds of problem.** Technical problems (fingering, movement, speed, dynamics, correct rhythm) are solved HS; coordination is solved HT. Do not attack both together. HS is also the safe investigation channel, because HT is where hand memory forms and wrong things stick ([T3085 r17][T3085], [T4858 r9][T4858]).
- **HS is not obligatory.** If a first slow HT sight-read of a passage is fine, go straight to HT; if a passage fails, isolate it for HS. Some pieces need every bar HS, others none; he joins hands straight away for Mozart, many Chopin waltz passages, and beginner pieces ([T3085 r17][T3085], [T4797 r19, r20][T4797]). Hands-together from scratch is for sight-reading practice only ([T3085 r15][T3085]).
- **When HS is used, join as soon as HS is truly mastered**, often in the same session; do not wait for the next day. If HS is not clean, do not join ([T4858 r9, r33][T4858], [T4797 r20][T4797]).
- **Non-counterpoint cadence for overlapping chunks:** bars 1-4 HS then HT in the same session; bars 4-8 HS then HT; bars 1-8 HT only (no HS); bars 8-12 HS then HT; bars 1-12 HT ([T4797 r25][T4797]).
- **Counterpoint (Bach, fugues, inventions):** learn and memorise each voice separately, learn the whole piece HS before ever joining, and keep rehearsing voices separately even after mastery so each stays fresh in the ear. Fugue example: voices alone, then each hand alone (with the middle voice hopping between hands), then top plus middle voice with one hand "completing" it, then bass plus middle voice, then both hands. Fingering makes or breaks it; use dropping notes for the joins. Also useful: have a teacher play one hand while you play the other, or record one hand and play along ([T4710 r8][T4710], [T4797 r18][T4797]).
- **Exceptions to HS first:** pieces with a melody on top, a bass, and an arpeggiated middle voice shared by both hands (Grieg Arietta, Schumann "Of foreign lands and people", Mendelssohn op. 19/1): top and bass voices alone, but the shared middle voice must be HT at once. Rachmaninoff op. 23/5, first part: outline but keep HT; second part HS ([T4797 r19][T4797]).
- **Long fast passages:** practise each hand at speed at least 30% above the final speed; then HT will follow, and you can join at final tempo ([T4858 r8][T4858]).
- **Alternate hands in the stage-2 session.** Learn each hand briefly first (investigation, a few minutes), then alternate: 10-20 repeats of one hand at first, quickly down to a couple, finally one-one; not more than about 30 s on a hand at a time. Choose passages that load both hands equally; couple the hard right hand of one piece with the hard left hand of another (and the easy hands in a second session) to avoid overworking one hand ([T4858 r2, r8][T4858], [T5298 r1][T5298]).
- **Dropping notes** (the joining method). Prerequisite: both hands are thoroughly ingrained (you could hold a conversation while playing one). Play hand A in full and keep it flowing no matter what; add only the first note or chord of hand B; then the first two; and so on until B is complete. Priority order: hand A continuous and error-free first, accuracy of the dropped hand second (errors in B are tolerated early on). Then swap roles. Work in tiny passages (one or two bars; phrases are a better unit than bars). It is aimed at coordination (the hands stay independent yet coordinated), not at note-by-note matching of one hand's note with the other's. It works especially well for Bach. Sources: [T1651 r7][T1651], [T3085 r2][T3085], [T4858 r8][T4858], [T3039 r45][T3039].
- **Hear the other hand first.** If the sound of both hands together is clear in the ear, the fingers tend to comply; use a teacher or a recording to supply the other hand ([T3085 r2][T3085], [T4797 r18][T4797]).

### 2.6 Speed

**Stance in one paragraph.** There are two legitimate slow practices: (1) extremely slow playing as a memory test only; (2) "slow motion", slower than final but using exactly the movements you will use at speed, as slow as needed for accuracy. Ordinary slow practice done with whatever movements work at slow speed builds wrong habits and a speed wall well below the final tempo. A new piece should not be explored slowly at the piano; analyse, listen, plan, rehearse in the mind, then try. But a slow sight-read of a new piece is allowed as diagnosis, as long as it is not turned into hours of "practice" ([T3371 r7][T3371], [T4282 r3, r7][T4282], [T5298 r29][T5298]). Gradual increases are fine if the slow-motion movements are the right ones; he says you can simply alternate between slow motion and final tempo ([T4282 r11][T4282]).

- **Slow-motion practice** ([T4282 r3][T4282], [T3371 r7][T3371]): movements identical to those at speed; just slow enough for accuracy (it can still be fairly fast); only once the correct movement is known; never exploratory. A forum counter-view: slow practice is essential for accuracy and speed walls dissolve when the body re-organises ([T5298 r20][T5298]).
- **Very slow memory test:** play each note about 10 seconds apart (a metronome at 32 with one note per four beats gives the idea). This kills hand memory, so success proves you know the piece's harmony and structure; failure means more memorisation work. He dismisses stories that Rachmaninoff or Gould trained touch this way ([T3371 r7][T3371]).
- **Chord trick (chord attack).** Play two to five close notes together as a chord, which is the fastest possible, so you see the hand movement at speed; then "roll" or wiggle the chord into a rapid arpeggio and slow it down to the final tempo. Works for up to five notes without skips. For arpeggios group by arpeggio, not by beat. The real speed limit is the lateral hand displacement between groups, so practise the two notes either side of every jump, then groups as chords, then break the chords. It finds the movement; it is not the only way ([T3371 r8][T3371], [T5298 r1, r10][T5298]).
- **Other ways to find the right movement.** Think and experiment; watch a slow-motion video of someone who plays it well (model, do not imitate); move from the centre and let the arm move the fingers, but break that rule when needed; ideal movement is relative to the whole phrase, not the first two notes; if you cannot find it, it is the teacher's job ([T5298 r1, r10][T5298], [T3561 r12][T3561], [T4282 r3][T4282]).
- **Repeated note-groups (the routine he calls most powerful).**
  - Procedure: take a passage of N units (notes, chords, beats or bars). Master every overlapping pair of units (1-2, 2-3, ...), then every overlapping triple (1-2-3, 2-3-4, ...), then quadruples, and so on until the group spans the whole passage. Repeat each group until it feels easy (no fixed repeat count), practising more where it is hard and less where it is easy.
  - Size: ideal about 7 units (30-45 min); 5 is the smallest, about 12 the maximum. To save time use bigger units (a bar, a beat or a quintuplet instead of a note), and stop early once the first and last groups overlap (in 15 units, at 8-note groups the first and last overlap), then try the whole passage; if that fails go up one size. Sources: [T4858 r9][T4858], [T3371 r8][T3371], [T5298 r1][T5298].
  - Rules: finish the whole set in one session (it overrides the 20-min cap); alternate hands per group to avoid strain; usually a one-off, never more than three days for any passage; start with octaves for chord passages to nail the skips, then restore the inner notes; the same method works for chords as units ([T4858 r9][T4858], [T4858 r8 Malaguena][T4858]).
  - Purpose: not only speed; it also teaches, memorises, builds hand memory, fingering and movement. Use it on truly impossible passages; a beginner may need it for every bar, an experienced player rarely (he needed it on every 7-note group for his first Scarlatti sonata, almost never after about ten sonatas) ([T5298 r1, r10][T5298]).
  - Common sins of students: they avoid it, avoid overlap, avoid small sections ([T4797 r15][T4797]).
- **Rhythm variations (RV).**
  - Procedure: for passages of equal note values, regroup into pairs and play fast-slow (play the first note immediately, hold the second as long as you like, never hesitate on the fast one), then the mirror slow-fast; for harder cases use three-note patterns and all their rotations (slow-slow-fast, slow-fast-slow, fast-slow-slow) and fast-fast-slow variants. Strict rhythmic placement of the slow note is optional; it tends to even out by itself.
  - Uses: evenness in long runs, curing a hesitation, forcing memory (the hard direction exposes weak knowledge), and discovering the passage's natural accent pattern: the pattern that feels easy follows the phrase, the hard mirror image accents non-melodic notes. Principle: practise the opposite of what you need (evenness through unevenness, speed through slow motion, lyricism through fast playing).
  - When: after the notes and movement are sorted out, as a tool for perfecting, not for learning notes. The same idea applies to accent and articulation variations (staccato versus legato). A Für Elise example (bars 53-54): scale with repeated Gs; practise the scale with the final fingering, then add the Gs, playing them lightly. Sources: [T5298 r1, r8, r9, r10, r25][T5298].
- **Tempo targets.** Always practise a bit faster than needed so the final tempo feels effortless (a reserve of speed, a favourite trick of Arrau's, even for slow sections); if you cannot exceed the final tempo, practise as fast as you can but perform slower; in performance the music should look and feel easy. The weaker hand's HS speed bounds the hands-together speed at roughly 70% of it (to play HT at MM 120, make the weaker hand reach about MM 172). Once the HS target is reached, join hands straight at final tempo ([T4858 r7][T4858], [T3371 r8][T3371]).
- **Speed is partly an illusion:** when every note is heard clearly the ear is overloaded and it sounds fast; accurate and clear slow playing can sound faster than blurred fast playing ([T5298 r1][T5298], [T3371 r8][T3371]). Speed also rises on its own from repetition: measure your tempo at the start and the end of a 15-minute block; it can double or triple unnoticed.
- **Metronome and speed.** Do not ramp up in equal steps; jump up and back, for example 60-92-80-100-92-112-100-132-112-152-120. Use a metronome sparingly for dead-tone reasons (Section 2.11), and see Section 2.11 for when it is mandatory ([T5298 r1][T5298]).
- **Speed walls and clustering.** A speed wall is a limit of the movement, not of muscles: do not push the same movement, switch to a different one (needs hands-on teaching). Think of changing gear: group notes into larger chunks until the whole passage is one unit ([T5298 r1][T5298], [T4282 r3][T4282]).
- **Worked method for a fast arpeggio passage** (Moonlight finale bars 1-2, in Section 3).

### 2.7 Outline, analysis, desk work, fingering

- **Work away from the piano first; he puts the share at about 90%.** Listen to several recordings with the score, do a harmonic reduction and motif analysis, tap the rhythm, sing the voices, rewrite the score (separate voices for counterpoint, motif-only score, outline), write the final fingering, then rehearse it in the mind and feel the fingering before touching keys. Give a talk about the piece to friends so the analysis sticks. Sources: [T3371 r7][T3371], [T3039 r49][T3039], [T7810 r12][T7810].
- **Outline** means strip the small notes (arpeggio figuration, ornaments) and keep the melodic or structural notes; learn the outline first and add figuration back later. He uses it for Chopin Op. 10 No. 2 and Op. 25 No. 1, Alkan Op. 39 No. 4 (index entry), and the melody-plus-arpeggio sections of Scherzo no. 2; with an outline played HT you can work on musicality while the technique is still trivial. For difficult chord passages, first learn octaves only to nail the skips, then restore the inner notes ([T4689 notes][T4689], [T7810 r22][T7810], [T4858 r8][T4858], [T5767 index][T5767]).
- **Analyse repeats before cutting into chunks.** Make a map of identical or near-identical bars (he did this with a table for the 781-bar Scherzo no. 2); practise the unique bars once and treat the copies as free, rehearsing only the small differences. Use photocopies and scissors if needed ([T7810 r22, r25][T7810]).
- **Final fingering from the start;** fingering implies movement; Bach and Scarlatti demand long fingering investigation; fingering problems usually yield quickly with correct repetition; for Couperin's Tic Toc Choc a 20-minute fingering session came before anything else ([T5298 r29][T5298], [T3561 r12][T3561], [T4797 r20][T4797]).
- **Diagnose difficulty by sight-reading.** Read through the piece hands together at a comfortable tempo; whatever you cannot play at tempo and perfectly is a passage to isolate. Sight-reading does not teach the piece (students who sight-read for hours get nowhere), but it tells you what you face: easy pieces can be learned in a single read, and appearance on paper misleads (Chopin's Military Polonaise looks hard but is easy; much Baroque looks easy but is not) ([T3561 r12][T3561], [T5298 r29][T5298]).
- **Name the cause of the difficulty:** unreadable score (rewrite it), unfamiliar idiom (listen, do not drill), fingering (repeat the right one), physical (hand size, reach), tempo ([T3561 r12][T3561]). Videoing yourself and a good player and comparing is recommended.
- **Musicality is part of practice from the first size-appropriate chunk,** not a late phase; musical decisions are dynamics and agogics, the only parameters a pianist truly controls (tone colour is dynamics plus note length) ([T4797 r25][T4797], [T5298 r1][T5298], [T3039 r42][T3039]).
- **Sight-reading skills** (for completeness): it is a bundle of skills (hear from the page, see the page as a keyboard map, short-term memorise, find keys by touch, play by ear, recognise patterns not notes, all in order and apparently at once); pattern recognition and theory carry most of the progress; relative pitch is required, perfect pitch is not ([T2751 r11, r14][T2751]).

### 2.8 Memory

- Memory is association; a memory is retrieved only if it was stored with full attention. Five separate memories: aural (how it sounds), visual (the score and the keyboard), touch (finding keys by feel, eyes closed or in the dark), hand memory (endless correct repetition HT, fast but fragile: one slip and you restart, the main cause of blackouts), and "music" memory (harmony, structure, motifs). Work on whichever is weak, then integrate ([T2083 r1][T2083]).
- Test routine: leave the score on a desk away from the piano; play from memory; when you stall, go to the score, see why, return. It is mentally intense; lazy memorisation fails ([T2083 r1][T2083]).
- Hand memory only is not enough; have the very slow test (Section 2.6) and a harmonic map as a safety net ([T3371 r7][T3371]).
- Vary the way you play it so you are not tied to one cue: looking at keys, not looking, eyes on the score ([T4858 r41][T4858]).
- For Bach he points to a dozen-a-day memorisation thread and a French suite allemande example (not read) ([T4105 r12][T4105]).

### 2.9 Repertoire choice and progression

- **Volume.** About 20 pieces a year (100 in five years) as a floor, with "mastered" defined as ready to perform: right notes, memorised, reasonable interpretation, not final. Equivalent to 5-10 hours of music in 5-10 years (about 100-200 three-minute pieces) per Chang, and he agrees ([T4710 r1][T4710], [T4750 r14-r20][T4750]). The first two years are slowest; later learning speeds up because music is heavily patterned ([T4750 r20][T4750]). He cites professional repertoire sizes (Richter about 1,500, Arrau close to 1,000, most pros 500+, Brendel about 150 public) and Browning demanding a sonata a week; these are his statements, not checked here.
- **Why many pieces and not a few:** (1) technique comes from pieces so you need diverse repertoire; (2) beginners cannot stay interested in one piece for a year; (3) motivation and something to perform; (4) different pieces need different learning strategies; (5) work on one piece informs another; (6) neglect-and-relearn needs spare pieces to work on in the gaps; (7) after thoroughly learning 5-6 Scarlatti sonatas or 3-4 fugues the rest come easily. Sources: [T4750 r14, r15, r18][T4750], [T4105 r8][T4105].
- **Progressive order.** Arrange the 100 pieces in increasing difficulty so each gives the technical and musical resources for the next; an experienced teacher is valuable here ([T4710 r1][T4710]). The index says [T4750] contains a progressive repertoire list leading to a Rachmaninoff prelude; that list is on a later page I did not read.
- **Use easy repertoire** deliberately: MacDowell "To a Wild Rose" (about 20 min for a Liszt-level player, two weeks for a beginner), Scarlatti K32, Hovhaness "Sleeping Cat", Shostakovich "Dance of the Dolls"; easy pieces need no tricks and are real music ([T4750 r16][T4750], [T3561 r2][T3561], [T5298 r29][T5298]).
- **Play what you love.** He rejects choosing repertoire because it is good for you (the broccoli argument); taste changes as you play; do not tie learning to exams or competitions as the end goal ([T4105 r8][T4105], [T4710 r12][T4710]).
- **Maintenance by performing and by planned forgetting.** A mastered piece is kept alive by performing it, rotating a performance list so each gets equal turns. To get omniscience, deliberately neglect for 6-12 months, relearn from scratch, repeat 3-5 times; write the manual of how you learned it and follow it each time to prevent shortcuts ([T7810 r2][T7810], [T4168 r3, r6][T4168]).
- **Spacing by stage:** stage-1 pieces (impossible) need daily work or they are forgotten; stage-2 pieces can be spaced ([T4105 r12][T4105]). From earlier notes: a mastered piece needs only two or three plays a week.
- **Technique from pieces, not drills.** Choose pieces that cover different techniques; skip Hanon; Czerny or Scarlatti are fine if they are treated as music; scales and arpeggios are needed as the foundation of key knowledge, practised from the key of your current piece in clusters, not as isolated exercises ([T7810 r0 items 7-13][T7810], [T19043 r2, r23][T19043]).

### 2.10 Practice diary and scheduling tools

- **Keep a practice journal.** It lists how you are learning each piece in detail: start and end times, the chunk, the tricks used, where you think the difficulty is, feelings (how hard or impossible it seems, which you can read back after two weeks when it is mastered), and a what-to-do-next note, because tomorrow you will forget. Stay concise so the diary does not eat the practice time. He also keeps cut-up score copies of the sections and their joins in it. Sources: [T4797 r16][T4797], [T4105 r8][T4105], [T4710 r1][T4710], [T7810 r0][T7810].
- **A checklist of steps** (suggested by a forum member, endorsed by Bernhard) prevents the commonest failing: skipping steps on day 2. Write the steps on day 1, tick them on day 2 ([T4797 r6, r16][T4797]).
- **Per-session template used by a student, accepted as fine:** Goal, Steps, Conclusion (did we meet the goal, and if not why, and what is the fix), then tomorrow's session copied or adapted ([T4858 r35, r43][T4858]).
- **Where students go wrong (from his teaching experience):** guessing notes by ear and button-pressing instead of reading; avoiding repeated note-groups; wanting HT at once; avoiding overlap and small sections; stopping at the first right rendition instead of the seven repeats; and rebelling against redoing day 1's steps on day 2 ([T4797 r15][T4797]).
- **Minimum frequency:** three sessions a week; consistency is the key; the day-of-week pattern may vary ([T4797 r21][T4797], [T7810 r0][T7810]).
- **Treat each variable as an experiment** and compare with a similar piece ([T4797 r25][T4797]).

### 2.11 Exercises, scales, metronome, and logical systems

- **No abstract technical exercises.** Hanon, Dohnanyi and Cortot's Rational Principles book are dismissed as a waste built on bad anatomy (high finger lifting, strengthening finger muscles that do not exist). Exercises inside a musical context are fine: the Cortot editions of Chopin and Schumann, with preliminary exercises tied to a passage, are the kind he approves of (but Cortot changes Chopin's fingerings and some notes). Etudes by Chopin, Liszt and Czerny are fine if you want the music ([T19043 r2, r10, r23][T19043]). This matters for the app: any drill should be tied to a specific passage in a piece, and chosen because of a diagnosed problem.
- **Problem-first, not method-first.** He refused to publish a single logical system because such systems are personal and only practical solutions to specific problems generalise: first identify the real problem (what exactly can you not do?), then try tools and see what works. Learn the piece in a dispersive way (listen, concerts, the composer's life, the autograph, analysis) and do not just sit at the piano ([T5298 r28, r29][T5298]).
- **Metronome.** Mandatory when a student cannot tell rhythm from pulse and for building an inner time sense in real time; used briefly at the tiny-section stage to make hands exact in relative duration (Military Polonaise bar 29: hands matched at tempo, then rhythm made ironclad; Malaguena step 3). Not for long periods because music cannot be brought to life with an even pulse; he measured a digital piano's recorded Debussy Arabesque at pulses between about 34 and 220 and a Bach Invention between 83 and 89, so Romantic pulse flexes widely. Three student stages: no pulse (metronome required), then steady pulse (drop the metronome), then fine control of expanding and contracting pulse (no metronome). The score is not the music (he quotes Harnoncourt) ([T12075 r1, r5, r7, r11][T12075], [T3039 r43][T3039], [T4858 r8][T4858]).
- **Thumb under versus thumb over** (scales): thumb over is a coordinated set of four motions (shift, slant, rotation, back and forth); once found it needs no drilling ([T19043 r32][T19043]).

## 3. Worked examples he gives

All are examples of the procedure, not fixed prescriptions; he says other students need smaller or larger steps.

### 3.1 Chopin Op. 10 No. 2 (carried from earlier notes, topic 4689 msg44184)

Outline score (chords only) plus a separate figuration score; final fingering from the start; desk work (rewriting the score) counts as practice; at most 2-3 minutes at a time on the chromatic figuration (injury risk); repeated note-groups using 5-semiquaver units that overlap by one, growing until the groups span the two bars; then rhythm variations. Session plan: S1 bars 1-2 (+ beat 1 of bar 3); S2 bars 3-4 (+ beat 1 of bar 5); S3 bars 1-4; S4 bars 5-6; S5 bars 1-6, so every other session is a join. He also recommended Cortot's Edition de travail preliminary exercises for this etude.

### 3.2 Satie, Gymnopedie 1: 10 sessions, one per day ([T4710 r1, r8][T4710])

| Session | Bars (with overlap) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1-12 (+ beat 1 of 13) |
| 2 | 1-21 (+ beat 1 of 22) (printed as 1-21; may mean adding 13-21 to the earlier 1-12) |
| 3 | 22-26 |
| 4 | 1-26 |
| 5 | 26-33 |
| 6 | 1-33 |
| 7 | 33-39 (+ beat 1 of 40) |
| 8 | 1-39 (first part complete) |
| 9 | 72-78 |
| 10 | 1-78 (whole piece; bars 40-71 reuse 1-32) |

Session 1 content in about 15 min: LH bars 1-2 alone (they recur six times in 12 bars), name and hear the chords (Gmaj7, Dmaj7), practise the big bass skips and chord voicing; then RH bars 5-9 (fingering, note sequence, memorise); the D major scale over two octaves and also the Phrygian mode, because the melody uses that mode; then join hands with the dropping-notes tricks. If a student only manages HS on day 1, join HT on day 2. Day 2: redo session 1 in full (2-3 min instead of 15), then session 2. Day 4: all sessions combined; with a section this size, begin phrasing, dynamics and agogics (musicality is never left to the end). An experienced player: 10-15 min total.

### 3.3 A twelve-session practice day ([T4710 r1][T4710])

S1 first two bars of Chopin op. 10/1 HT; S2 outline of Op. 25/1; S3 first arpeggio figuration of Op. 25/1; S4 ornaments of Scarlatti K427 worked out away from the piano; S5 performance run of Schubert Impromptu op. 90/3 (learned last month); S6 Bach fugue in G minor (WTC 2 no. 15), each voice separately, bars 3-5; S7 sight-read Part's "Spiegel im Spiegel"; S8 op. 10/1 bars 25-26 HT; S9 Beethoven op. 78 performance run; S10 more of S9; S11 op. 10/1 bars 76-79 HT; S12 play for pleasure. ### 3.4 Bach Invention no. 1: motifs, then hands separate, then hands together ([T7664 r0, r1, r3][T7664])

Stated as 20-40 daily sessions for someone new to this style. His scheme is three passes of different content over the same bars:

| Pass | Sessions | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Motif score (rewrite the piece as motif and variations only) | 10 | 1: bars 1-2; 2: 3-4; 3: 11-12; 4: 1-4 and 11-12; 5: 5 and 7-10; 6: 1-12; 7: 13 and 15-18; 8: 1-18; 9: 19-21; 10: 1-21 |
| Hands separate (each with +1 beat overlap) | 14 | 1: 1-2; 2: 3-4; 3: 5-6; 4: 1-6; 5: 7-10; 6: 1-10; 7: 11-12; 8: 1-12; 9: 13-14; 10: 1-14; 11: 15-18; 12: 1-18; 13: 19-22; 14: 1-22 |
| Hands together, built from the end backwards | 15 | 1: 19-22; 2: 15-18; 3: 15-22; 4: 13-14; 5: 13-22; 6: 11-12; 7: 11-22; 8: 7-10; 9: 7-22; 10: 5-6; 11: 6-22 (as printed, probably 5-22); 12: 3-4; 13: 3-22; 14: 1-2; 15: 1-22 |

Totals 39 sessions: 39 days at one session a day. Bar counts are as printed in the thread (the motif pass says 21 bars, the others 22).

Execution rules from the replies: master a session before moving on; session 6 after sessions 4 and 5 is the next day's work unless you are in a hurry. With several sessions a day, joins (shown in square brackets in his schedule) are quick, so a day can hold one new session plus one or two quick joins. His 18-day schedule has days of 2-4 sessions with the long joins (the whole motif score, the whole HS piece) re-run every day for a few minutes; his 12-day plan does the same with up to 5 sessions a day and is not recommended. By the time the final hands-together session is reached, the whole motif score has been run about 29 times and the whole hands-separate piece about 15 times, each only a couple of minutes (about 3 run-throughs each is his ideal). After the last session, the technique is done and the work becomes musicality; hands-separate work can be dropped, though he still reads through the motif score now and then to remember what the piece is about.

### 3.5 Short pieces in 6-8 sessions each ([T7810 r1, r2][T7810])

- C.P.E. Bach Fantasia in D minor (Wq 117/12): S1 bars 5-6 (hardest); S2 1-2; S3 2-5; S4 1-6; S5 6-9; S6 1-9; S7 10-14; S8 1-14.
- Blow, Sarabande in C: S1 1-4; S2 5-8; S3 1-8; S4 9-12; S5 13-16; S6 1-16 (all bars equally hard, so go in order).
- Chopin, Cantabile: S1 3-4 (+ beat 1 of 5; bar 3 is the hardest); S2 1-2 (+ beat 1 of 3); S3 1-5; S4 5-8 (+ beat 1 of 9); S5 1-8; S6 9-14; S7 1-14.

His daily-table version: three pieces, about an hour per day split into three 20-minute sessions, each with 2-3 minutes of review and 15-18 minutes of the new session. He also gave a hypothetical "what actually happens" table over ten days, with typical deviations: the hard first session needs whole sessions for relearning; a join session is repeated because the hard passage is still shaky; some days a join session also includes extra work on the hard part; finally the hard part is absorbed into a later join. Because the hard passage is tackled first, it naturally gets practised the most. He added: keep the piece alive daily until month end, then replace it; mastered pieces are practised by performing.

### 3.6 A 30-bar piece with a hard section ([T5298 r1][T5298])

Two sessions per day for this piece. Day 1: bars 25-26 (hardest), and bars 1-5. Day 2: 25-26, and 5-10. Day 3: 25-26, and 1-10. Day 4: 25-26, and 10-15. Day 5: 25-26, and 1-15. Day 6: 25-26 finally mastered, and 15-20. Day 7: 25-30 (27-30 are easy) and 1-20. Day 8: 20-25 and the whole piece.

### 3.7 Chopin Scherzo no. 2: first 264 bars in seven sessions ([T7810 r22, r25][T7810])

A student had split it into 79 sessions; he cut the first 264 bars to seven: (1) bars 1-48 using bars 1-9, 14-17 (near copy of 6-9, repeated-note-groups on its five chords), 18-24, then join 1-24; bars 25-48 are an almost exact repeat with differences in 30-33, 38-41, 42-48; (2) bars 49-65 HS first, because 49-52 equals 57-60 and the RH of 53-56 equals 61-64, and the LH of 62-64 is the same figuration an octave lower (alternate hands); (3) bars 65-96 in three workouts (outline of melody plus first note of each arpeggio; LH figurations by repeated note-groups, one bar as a unit, 8 bars at a time, 45-60 min each, minimum six days; then HT using dropping notes); (4) bars 96-117; (5) bars 117-130; (6) bars 1-130 joining; (7) bars 1-264 including the repeats. The second round showed how to fold exact repeats using a bar-equivalence table: one session on bars 265-268 masters 28 bars (it recurs seven times), and bars 400-467 repeat 298-365. His advice: learn the method on 16-32 bar pieces first.

### 3.8 Lecuona, Malaguena (a hands-separate session) ([T4858 r8][T4858])

For a beginner with weak technique. Analyse repetition (the left hand has two groups of chords; only bars 2-4 need learning, nine chords). Use repeated note-groups: two-chord groups first with octaves instead of full chords (to nail the skips), then restore the middle notes; stop and switch hands when the working hand tires. For the right hand ignore rhythm at first (treat all chords as equal) and learn six chords in sequence; alternate hands per group. Once smooth, add a metronome to make hand durations equal and rhythm iron-clad. Learn the whole line HS (repeating each chord several times helps avoid hesitation). After a 5 minute break, a second session joins hands: a short HS check, then dropping notes (RH full, drop LH; then LH full, drop RH), 5-10 min. The remaining 10 minutes: musicality (accents, articulation, a clear mental image of the sound). Next day go straight to HT; if forgotten, redo every step.

### 3.9 Chopin Military Polonaise bars 29-30 ([T3039 r43][T3039])

(1) Work on bar 29 plus the first chord of bar 30 for overlap. (2) Split into triplets and ascending quaver chords. (3) Triplets HS seven times each hand (10-15 s). (4) Metronome to match hand durations at speed; 15-20 min or less. (5) Drop it for the day. (6) Ascending chords HS: 1-2, 2-3, 3-4, 4-5, then 123, 234, 345, then 1234, 2345, then 12345. (7) Next day repeat the same steps, probably faster. (8-9) Repeat until each hand is right straightaway, typically 2-3 days, never more than seven. (10) Join hands, each part 15-20 min, using the same 7-rule. Over about a week at 5-20 min/day, versus a 5-hour single session which would ingrain errors and leave nothing consolidated.

### 3.10 Beethoven Moonlight finale, bars 1-2 RH (speed) ([T3371 r8][T3371])

Regroup the notes by arpeggio, not by written beat; number the seven arpeggios (the last has three notes). Play each arpeggio as a chord at once. Practise just the jump between each arpeggio's endpoints (G#-C#, C#-E, E-G# ...), then whole arpeggios as chords, then break chords into notes and slow to final tempo while finding the cheapest movement. Use rhythm variations for evenness and accent variations for tone. When the movement is secure do repeated groups over the arpeggios: 1..7 singly, then 12, 23, ... 67, then 123 ... 567, then larger, taking 15-20 min in all. Same for the left hand (alternate from the start), then HT, then the whole piece in 2-3 bar sections.

### 3.11 Bach Prelude in F# minor, WTC 1 ([T3039 r49][T3039])

Before the piano: listen to several pianists and harpsichordists, motif analysis, harmonic reduction, score copy with the three voices isolated and fingering written, sight-read to find the hard bars. Then a 5-hour day is cut into 12 sessions of 20 minutes, each on a different bar. Session 1 finds the chunk size: bars 1-4 HS fail the 7-repeat test, so two bars fail too, and one bar passes at about the fifth repeat. Session 2 (after a break) masters that bar: a few minutes of HS with hundreds of quick repeats, rhythm variations and chord versions, then the rest of the session HT, the bar finishing memorised and nearly performable. Day 2: it feels like never seen but is relearned in about 5 minutes; day 3 about 1 minute; day 4 perfect at the first try. Across days 1-3 the 12 sessions of day 1 cover about 12 bars, day 2 adds about six more, and day 3 finishes the last six and starts joining 2-3 bar passages. Estimate: 4-5 days to master and memorise the 24-bar prelude, even for someone with no technique, with technique acquired section by section.

### 3.12 Bach Prelude and Fugue in C minor, WTC 2 ([T4710 r8][T4710])

Prelude (treat like a two-voice invention): stage 1 write and learn a motif-only score; stage 2 add the missing notes, learn each hand separately; stage 3 join. Fugue (four voices, only three until bar 18): five stages (voices alone with final fingering, each hand alone, top plus middle voice, bass plus middle voice, full) with a complete analysis first; use dropping notes for joins.

### 3.13 Two reading experiments ([T5298 r29][T5298])

Hovhaness "Sleeping Cat": read on a bus, then sight-read once, play it once from memory, never practise it again (easy for him). Couperin "Tic Toc Choc": the first sight-read showed it was hell; the first 20-minute session went to fingering of section A only, because A recurs three times and was more than half the piece. Lesson: the first read decides what to do.

### P1: the practice plan core (data the app already has)

- **I1 Seven-pass chunk probe.** User picks a loop (default one phrase or 2-4 measures, hands separate). Verdict: "fits" if the last few passes are clean (no wrong, no missed) and trending better; "too big" if pass 7 is not clearly cleaner than pass 1 or has errors, and then proposes halving at the nearest measure or beat down to 2 notes. Does not award practice credit; it only measures. Calibrate the "7": store each user's median passes-to-first-clean per chunk and offer it as a personal number (he says 7 is only an average, SD about 2). Optional last pass with the sheet hidden to test "memorised". Data: HAVE (per-pass judgment). Hide-sheet toggle: NEW but trivial. ### P2: techniques as modes and drills

- **I7 Dropping-notes join mode.** One hand is "strict" and plays continuously; the other hand's required notes are revealed gradually (first note, first two, ...). The judge ignores errors in the dropped hand at first and counts stalls only in the strict hand; then swap roles. Needs play-along (non-stopping), not wait-for-me. Option: the app plays the dropped hand's part itself while the user plays one hand (Bernhard suggested a teacher or a recording supplying the other hand). Data: HAVE (hand-wise judgment); NEW (per-note required/optional mask). Basis: section 2.5; [T1651 r7][T1651], [T3085 r2][T3085], [T4797 r18][T4797].
- **I8 Alternate-hands timer and pairing.** Auto-alternate hand selection every few passes or 30 seconds; cap per hand. In the plan, rank chunks by per-hand error rate and pair a hard-RH chunk from one piece with a hard-LH chunk from another in the same session (his Op. 10/2 with Op. 10/12 idea). Data: HAVE. Basis: section 2.5; [T4858 r2, r7][T4858], [T5298 r1][T5298].
- **I9 Repeated-note-group builder (his top tool).** On a loop, choose the unit (note, chord, beat, bar). The app produces the overlapping group list (pairs, triples, ... up to the full passage), runs each group as a short loop until k clean passes at the target tempo ("until easy" in his terms; k user-set), and moves on. Shows the size warning (5-12 units ideal) and offers coarser units; offers the early exit when the first and last group overlap ("try the whole passage"); alternates hands per group in HS; starts with octave-only or block-chord version for skip-heavy chords; exempt from the 20-min cap but must finish in the session (otherwise flagged incomplete). Marks the passage "group-set done"; re-offer only up to 3 days. Data: HAVE (per-pass); NEW (loop granularity below the measure). Basis: section 2.6; [T4858 r9][T4858], [T3371 r8][T3371], [T5298 r1, r10][T5298].
- **I10 Variation drills with mirror enforcement.** Map the existing drills: dotted rhythm = fast-slow; add its mirror (slow-fast) automatically and the three-note rotations; the judge demands the fast note arrive with no hesitation and is lenient on the held note. Accent drill: use velocity contrast on alternating notes; articulation drill: staccato versus legato from note-off times. Only offer after the chunk has been learned (these are perfecting tools, not learning tools), and only on equal-note runs. Show which direction was harder: his insight that the hard mirror reveals the phrase's accent pattern. Data: HAVE (timing, velocity); NEW (note-off for articulation). Basis: section 2.6; [T5298 r1, r8, r10][T5298].
- **I11 Problem-first tool selector.** Ask "what is going wrong?" and map it. Hesitation or unevenness -> rhythm variations; inaccurate skips -> repeated groups with octave first; speed wall -> change movement/fingering, chord trick, groups; HT coordination -> dropping notes; memory blackouts -> memory modes (I13); tone evenness -> accent variations; can't find movement -> chord trick, slow-motion video (advice); unfamiliar idiom -> listen first (advice). Follows his repeated "start from the problem" rule and avoids drills for their own sake. Data: HAVE (error patterns can suggest the answer), mostly ADVICE. Basis: section 2.11; [T5298 r10, r28][T5298].
- **I12 Tempo policy.** Practice target at or above final tempo ("reserve"), with the app showing perform-tempo below practice-tempo if reserve not reached; a non-monotonic tempo ladder (alternate between lower and higher, like 60-92-80-100-92-112...) scaled to the user's range; HS targets derived from the HT target (HS weaker-hand tempo about HT tempo divided by 0.7); a speed-wall detector (max clean tempo plateau over sessions) that tells the user to change movement, not push; a chord-attack quality check (all notes of a block within a few ms) for the block-chords drill; record tempo at start and end of each block to show the hidden gain. Data: HAVE. Caveat: the app cannot see whether the movement is the right one; slow versus slow-motion stays ADVICE. Basis: section 2.6, 2.11; [T3371 r7, r8][T3371], [T4858 r7][T4858], [T5298 r1][T5298], [T12075 r1][T12075].
- **I13 Memory modes.** Hidden-sheet pass; ultra-slow memory test (about 10 s per note, in wait-for-me); "score on the desk" mode (view the sheet only on request, with request count as a metric); suggested non-piano tasks in the plan (listening, harmonic map, copying out the score). Data: HAVE; NEW (hide sheet, request counter). Basis: section 2.8; [T2083 r1][T2083], [T3371 r7][T3371], [T4858 r41][T4858].
- **I14 HS/HT recommender per chunk.** From the diagnostic pass: if HT is clean, skip HS; if one hand fails, HS for that hand; counterpoint tag forces voice/hand separation for the whole piece; shared-middle-voice textures stay HT; long fast runs: HS target 30% over final. Never offer HT while HS is not clean. Data: HAVE; counterpoint detection NEW (score-level). Basis: section 2.5; [T3085 r17][T3085], [T4797 r19, r20][T4797], [T4858 r8][T4858].

### P3: planning at scale and nice-to-have

- **I15 Repertoire planner.** 5-year list, progressive order by a difficulty score (note density, span, hand independence, tempo), monthly sets, long-term relearn calendar (leave 6-12 months, then relearn in full). Needs a curated catalogue with difficulty metadata; otherwise ADVICE. Suggest easy repertoire between hard pieces. Basis: section 2.9; [T4710 r1][T4710], [T4750 r14, r15][T4750], [T4168 r3][T4168].
- **I16 Performance rotation and run-through mode.** Non-stopping full-piece mode, counter of performances (he says about the third is when you are ready), rotating list so mastered pieces are maintained by performing. Data: HAVE. Basis: section 2.9, 2.2; [T4105 r12][T4105], [T7810 r2][T7810].
- **I17 Score-processing helpers.** Outline generator (drop short ornamental notes, keep first note of arpeggio groups), voice separation view, duplicate-bar map and highlighter, "final fingering locked" editor. Data: NEW (score analysis). Basis: section 2.7; [T7810 r22, r25][T7810], [T4689].
- **I18 Musicality feedback.** Dynamics (velocity curve) and agogics (timing, note length) are the pianist's only levers per him; show them as phrase-shape curves versus a reference or the user's own previous best; offer a strict pulse only in early small chunks, never as a permanent grade (his metronome stages). Evenness (low velocity and timing variance on equal-note runs) may serve as a proxy for "easy"; experimental. Data: HAVE (velocity, timing); the reference curve is NEW or user-supplied. Basis: section 2.7, 2.11; [T4797 r25][T4797], [T5298 r1][T5298], [T12075 r1][T12075].
- **I19 Sight-reading and reading skills.** Treat sight-reading as a separate training with look-ahead and pattern chunking; a mode that hides the keyboard so the eyes stay on the score; relative-pitch training. Data: HAVE (judgment) / NEW (modes). Basis: section 2.7; [T2751 r11, r14][T2751].
- **I20 Self-experiment log.** He repeatedly told readers to compare two pieces of similar difficulty, one learned his way and one not. A tag on each piece for the method used, with time-to-mastery and cold-test results, would let the user run the experiment. Data: HAVE plus the ledger. Basis: section 2.3; [T3039 r41, r43][T3039], [T4797 r25][T4797].

Design cautions from the sources: the app cannot see movement quality, hand memory formation, or sleep; all thresholds (7 passes, 20 minutes, 3-7 days, 70%, 30%) should be defaults the user or the app can tune; he insisted that the answer depends on the student and the piece, and that a plan must flex. Do not grade expressive timing against a metronome after the early stages. Keep drills tied to a specific passage.

## 5. Open questions and things he left vague

- What exactly counts as learned after seven repeats: correct and memorised (3039 r43, 4710), effortless (4858 r8), or also at speed (a student's reading, 4858 r0)? He never gives an error tolerance, so any pass-fail threshold in the app is our decision.
- At what tempo are the seven test repeats done: final, reduced, or whatever movement allows? Not stated; the chord trick is used to find a fast-motion movement before slowing down, but the probe itself is slow in several examples.
- Is the seven-repeat test HS or HT? He says investigation is HS (3039 r43, 4858 r8); for joined chunks he expects them to hold together by about the seventh repeat (4858 r8, 7664 r3). The question in 4710 r3 got no answer there.
- Cold-test leniency: instant, or perhaps after 4-5 repeats (4797 r20), or after 6-7 repeats (4858 r8), or judge by the trend (4105). No agreed rule.
- Interval rules are loose: one night's sleep is the minimum and complex tasks may need several nights; no maximum interval between sessions of the same chunk during the learning phase is given, only that stage-1 pieces need daily contact and omniscience uses 6-12 months of neglect.
- The statistics (7 repeats with SD 2; 4 days with SD 2; the 20-minute figure, which he himself calls a rough upper limit; HT about 37 times harder) have no published source; he says he sampled for the 7 but gives no data.
- Claims presented as facts that are his belief: overnight unconscious processing and dreaming, the absence of muscles in the fingers, the professional repertoire sizes, the claim that students reach 20 pieces a year, and that 700 repeats are no better than 7. Treat as hypotheses.
- Slow-motion practice has no numeric tempo guidance (as slow as accuracy needs, likened to tai chi in 3371 r7, and he says it can be fairly fast in 4282 r11), and the app cannot detect whether the movements are right.
- Easy is the target of all practice but is not defined by any measurement.
- The number of repeats for each group in a repeated note-group set is until it feels easy; the criterion is subjective.
- The best way to interleave pieces within a day, and the optimal number of parallel pieces (5-30 a month, 10 as an example), are left to the student.
- Dissent in the threads: critics said 20 pieces a year is unrealistic and that depth takes years (4750 r2, r4); one reader found that fast practice at 1.5 times speed thinned the tone and returned to gradual metronome increases (4282 r0); another forum reply said slow practice is essential and speed walls can dissolve spontaneously (5298 r20); Chang agreed with the general approach but estimated about a year per piece for a student with a teacher, 4-8 pieces a year, and that old pieces suffer when new ones are learned (4750 r7). Bernhard's answer is that his numbers mean performable, not finished, and that results depend on being organised.
- Still to read next (not read here; entries from the index 5767): 2714 (Invention 1 from scratch, the original long post), 2526 (short, medium and long-term organisation, memory retention), 4244 (defining an aim and moving on), 4004 (three right versus wrong attitudes), 2802 (when to join hands), 4123 (investigating movement, Scarlatti K70, CPE Bach Allegro), 3002 (when to use the metronome), 9285 (outlining Op. 25/1 with score), 9540 (learning a piece back to front), 7399 (a dozen-a-day memorisation example), 4851 (speed for Beethoven op. 49 no. 2), 4988 (his summary of planning links), and 2599 (dispersive learning).
